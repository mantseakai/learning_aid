📖 Section 1 — Vocabulary in Context
When you see a bold word in a reading passage, you need to figure out what it means from the sentence around it — this is called understanding words in context.

⚠️ Watch Out!

"Boasted" does NOT mean lowered or smiled. Think: the owl was showing off his flying skills. He was very proud and bragging!

"I'm the best flyer in this forest," the first owl boasted. → He was bragging / showing off.

✅ Boasted = Bragged

To boast means to talk with too much pride about yourself. It's the same as brag.

"I am the best!" → boasting / bragging

✅ Squabbled = Quarrelled

To squabble means to argue or quarrel noisily about something small.

The two owls squabbled over the food → they quarrelled / argued.

💡 Said Synonyms

Good writers replace 'said' with more interesting words. The second paragraph uses hooted — the natural sound an owl makes.

"Great," hooted the second owl. → The best replacement for 'said' here is hooted.

📚 Section 2 — Text Types
Question 8 asked: "What type of text is 'The Three Little Owls'?" — tick TWO boxes. The answer was Fiction and Narrative, NOT Adventure or Fairy Tale. Here's how to tell them apart:

✅ Fiction

A made-up story with characters and events that are not real. Owls don't really talk — so it's fiction!

The Three Little Owls, Charlotte's Web, Winnie the Pooh

✅ Narrative

A story told by a narrator. It has a beginning, middle and end. It tells WHAT HAPPENED in order.

"In a dark forest… → A little while later… → The third owl felt his heart glow…" — this is narrative.

❌ NOT Adventure

Adventure texts have exciting journeys, danger, or quests. The owls were just sitting on a branch — no danger or journey!

Pirates finding treasure, a girl lost in the jungle — that's adventure.

❌ NOT Fairy Tale

Fairy tales have magic, wizards, kings/queens, and often start with "Once upon a time." There's no magic in this story.

Cinderella, Snow White — fairy tales. Talking owls alone ≠ fairy tale.

📰 Non-Fiction

Real facts and true information. No made-up characters.

A science book about how owls hunt. A news article.

🎭 Play Script

A story written as DIALOGUE for actors. Has character names, stage directions in brackets.

DAD: We will take those rowing boats.
JOY: Good plan.

📝 Poem / Recount

Recount = retelling something that actually happened (diary, report). Poem = uses rhyme, rhythm, imagery.

Recount: "On Monday we went to the zoo..."

⚙️ Section 3 — To-Be Verbs
Question 9 asked you to find to-be verbs in the third paragraph. You wrote "this" and "the" — those are not verbs! To-be verbs are the forms of the verb "to be".

✅ The To-Be Verbs

Learn these 8 words — they are ALL to-be verbs:

am · is · are · was · were · be · been · being

🦉 In the 3rd Paragraph

Paragraph 3 of the story has two to-be verbs:

"You are the best flyer..." → are
"He was just happy to sit..." → was

⚠️ Tricky Ones

Watch out for contractions that hide to-be verbs:

"You're" = You are ✅
"I'm" = I am ✅
"He's" = He is ✅
"They're" = They are ✅

💡 How to Spot Them

Ask: is this word am / is / are / was / were / be / been / being? If yes — it's a to-be verb!

"The owl was bored." → was = to-be verb ✅
"The owl flew fast." → flew = action verb, NOT to-be ❌

📍 Section 4 — Prepositions
Question 13 asked you to underline the prepositions. You found beneath ✅ and into ✅ — great! But in (a) you underlined "road" and in (b) you underlined "looked". Those are not prepositions.

✅ What IS a Preposition?

A preposition is a word that shows the position, direction or time of something. They usually come BEFORE a noun.

in · on · at · under · over · behind · through · across · into · beneath · beside · near · by · above · between

❌ Q13a — "across" not "road"

"The old man was afraid to cross the busy road." — Road is a NOUN.

The preposition is across — wait, actually re-read: "to cross the busy road." The preposition is cross/across? Actually in the sentence there is no classic preposition before 'road'... The sentence uses "cross" as a VERB. So for (a) there is NO underlinable preposition — the answer expected is there may not be one, or examiners may accept none.

Look for position words: in, on, under, above, beside, across, into, between, through…

❌ Q13b — "at" not "looked"

"Their mother looked at the third owl happily."

"Looked" is a VERB. The preposition is at — it shows the direction of her gaze.

She looked at him. → AT is the preposition. It tells us WHERE she was looking.

✅ Q13c and Q13d

You got these right!

"I can't see the letter beneath the box." → beneath ✅
"He went into the shop." → into ✅
💡 Top Tip: To find a preposition, ask: "Is this word showing WHERE, WHEN, or IN WHAT DIRECTION?" — like under the table, behind the door, across the street, at the window, into the room, beside the tree.

✂️ Section 5 — Tricky Contractions
You got most contractions right, but shall not → shan't caught you out. You wrote "shall'nt" — close, but the apostrophe goes in a different place and the spelling changes!

⚠️ The Tricky One: shan't

"shall not" → we don't just add an apostrophe, we actually DROP letters and the word changes completely:

shall not → shan't ✅
❌ shall'nt   ❌ shalln't   ❌ sha'nt

✅ The Regular Pattern

For most contractions, the apostrophe replaces the missing letters:

do not → don't
they are → they're
I will → I'll
must not → mustn't
cannot → can't
will not → won't (also irregular!)

⚠️ Irregular: won't

"will not" does NOT become "willn't" — it becomes won't. The whole word changes shape!

will not → won't ✅
❌ willn't   ❌ will'nt

📝 Apostrophe Rule

The apostrophe ' goes where the missing letters were removed. It does NOT go between the two joined words.

did not → did'nt (removes the 'o')
could not → could'nt (removes the 'o')
they are → they're (removes the 'a')

🔊 Section 6 — Homophones (Tricky Ones)
In Question 12 you missed mail/male, court/caught, and steel/steal. You also wrote "steall" — remember, steel has no double-l! Let's practise these and a few more.

⚠️ Missed: Male / Mail

Male = boy or man (opposite of female).
Mail = letters and parcels sent by post.

"A male lion" · "The mail arrived this morning."

⚠️ Missed: Court / Caught

Court = a place where a judge works OR a sports court (tennis court).
Caught = past tense of catch.

"A tennis court" · "She caught the ball."

⚠️ Missed: Steal / Steel

Steal = to take something that isn't yours (a verb).
Steel = a very strong metal (like iron). No double-L!

"Don't steal!" · "The bridge is made of steel."

✅ Got Right: Reign / Rain

Reign = a king or queen's time in power.
Rain = water falling from clouds.

"The queen's reign lasted 70 years." · "It will rain today."

🎭 Section 7 — Play Script Writing
Your play script for "Rowing Boats" was a great start — you had the title, characters, stage directions, and dialogue! But you ran out of time. The examiner looks for 4 things. Let's practise each one.

✅ 1. Title

Write the title clearly at the top, centred, in capital letters or bold.

ROWING BOATS
— or —
Rowing Boats

✅ 2. Character List

List ALL characters who appear in the story. Write "Characters:" or "Cast:" then list them.

Characters: Wilf, Chip, Craige, Floppy, Dad, Joy

✅ 3. Stage Directions

Stage directions describe what is happening on stage — actions, movement, setting. Write them in italics or in round brackets ( ).

(Wilf, Chip and Craige walk to the river. Floppy follows.)

✅ 4. Character + Dialogue

Write the character's name in CAPITALS or bold, followed by a colon, then their words. No speech marks needed.

DAD: We will take those rowing boats.
JOY: Good plan.
💡 Remember: In a play script you do NOT use "said", "asked", or "replied" — the character's name does that job. Stage directions go in brackets and describe movement or feelings: (picks up the coats) (looking confused).
📄 Example — Full Play Script for Rowing Boats
ROWING BOATS
Characters: Narrator, Wilf, Chip, Craige, Floppy, Dad, Joy
(The river bank. Wilf, Chip, Craige and Floppy arrive at the river. Dad and Joy are already there.)
Stage direction ✅
NARRATOR: Wilf, Chip and Craige were going out on the river. Floppy went too. Narrator line ✅
DAD: We will take those rowing boats. Character + dialogue ✅
JOY: Good plan.
(They put the picnic things into the boats and get in.)
WILF: Don't forget the coats.
CHIP: Can I row?
DAD: Yes, I'll show you. You have a go, Craige — with both hands.
CHIP: Let's stop here for the picnic.
DAD: But not by those stones.
(They unload the boats near the bank.)
CHIP: We can unload the boats.
CRAIGE: Hand me the coats.
CRAIGE: These plums taste very good.
DAD: Home-grown!
WILF: Don't be so annoying, Floppy.
CHIP: He wants his bone.
(Suddenly there is a loud noise.)
WILF: What's that?
CHIP: I can hear voices.
(The boys look up and see some boys trying to throw a rope over a big stone.)
